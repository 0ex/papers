# Summary: Physical Probability in the Everett Interpretation and Bell Inequalities

**Simon Saunders (2026), PhilSci-Archive v3**

## Central claim

Saunders argues that Bell-inequality violations provide positive evidence for the Everett, or many-worlds, interpretation. His argument is that three assumptions cannot all be retained:

1. **LOC:** local physical probabilities cannot be changed by dynamically permitted actions at spacelike separation;
2. **measurement independence:** measurement choices are independent of the relevant prior physical state;
3. **unique outcomes:** remote measurements have exactly one actual outcome.

Together these imply Bell inequalities. Since experiments violate Bell inequalities, a theory that retains LOC and measurement independence must reject unique outcomes. Everettian quantum mechanics does so.

## LOC rather than Bell local causality

Saunders proposes **LOC**, a dynamical version of locality modelled on Bell's local-causality principle. Bell local causality compares conditional probabilities after specifying remote events. LOC instead asks whether an actual, dynamically allowed change in a spacelike-separated region changes a local single-case probability.

This distinction matters in Everettian theory. A remote measurement does not dynamically select one result; it creates branches containing all results. Conditioning on one remote branch may alter a conditional probability, but that is not the same as a distant physical action changing the local probability.

## Why unique outcomes generate the Bell constraint

In a one-world theory, LOC yields the familiar Bell conditions:

- **parameter independence:** Bob's choice of setting does not change Alice's outcome probabilities;
- **outcome independence:** Bob's actual outcome does not change Alice's conditional probabilities.

With measurement independence, these imply Bell inequalities. Standard collapse theories violate outcome independence through a real, unique remote outcome and state collapse. Pilot-wave theory instead accepts nonlocal dynamics.

In Everettian quantum mechanics, outcome independence can fail without a remote causal influence because every outcome occurs. Different conditional probabilities refer to different correlated branches, not to one distant outcome physically changing Alice's situation.

## The probability theory \(\lambda\)-MANY

Saunders develops an objective probability theory for Everettian mechanics called **\(\lambda\)-MANY**. A quantum state is represented using orthogonal equal-amplitude microstates. If an event contains \(m\) of \(n\) such microstates, its probability is \(m/n\).

For decompositions that do not align exactly with the event projector, the construction supplies upper and lower probability bounds. Under finer decompositions these bounds converge to the Born-rule value. The supporting principle, **INVARIANCE**, says that causal changes confined to an event disjoint from \(X\) cannot alter the physical probability of \(X\). In Hilbert-space terms, orthogonality and unitary invariance make equal-norm components equiprobable.

The microstates are probability-representing components, not hidden variables emitted by the source. Consequently, the decomposition may depend on the later measurement context without implying that a past physical state depends on a future choice.

## Parameter and outcome independence in \(\lambda\)-MANY

Bob's local measurement acts only on Bob's Hilbert-space factor. It therefore preserves the measure of Alice's outcome subspaces, so parameter independence holds.

For product states, the Alice and Bob microstate ensembles factorize, and outcome independence holds. For entangled states, the microstates are correlated non-factorizably. Conditioning on Bob's branch can therefore change Alice's conditional probability, so outcome independence fails. Saunders interprets this as a common-cause correlation established by the entangled preparation, not as action at a distance.

## Comparison with alternatives

Saunders contrasts \(\lambda\)-MANY with a hypothetical one-world theory, **\(\lambda\)-ONE**, in which only one microstate is real. To reproduce quantum predictions, the relevant microstate must depend on the eventual measurement context; the theory therefore gives up measurement independence through contextual, retrocausal, or superdeterministic structure.

The principal options after accepting Bell violations are thus:

- reject LOC and accept nonlocal dynamics;
- reject measurement independence;
- reject unique outcomes.

Saunders considers Everettian theory the best-developed theory occupying the third position. Because it retains local unitary dynamics and measurement independence while explaining the observed correlations, Bell violations are presented not merely as compatible with many worlds, but as evidence for it.

## Main points open to dispute

The argument depends on several substantive claims:

- LOC is the correct locality requirement, rather than Bell's stronger local-causality condition;
- branch-relative conditioning should not be treated as a distant physical intervention;
- the equal-amplitude microstate construction provides genuine objective probability;
- contextual probability representations do not themselves compromise measurement independence;
- rejecting unique outcomes is preferable to nonlocal, retrocausal, or superdeterministic alternatives.

The paper's distinctive contribution is to combine this locality argument with an explicit Everettian account of physical probability applicable directly to spacelike-separated events.
